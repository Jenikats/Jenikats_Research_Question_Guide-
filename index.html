Foundation

What Is a Research Question?

The central question a study is designed to answer — it connects topic, problem, evidence, and argument.

A well-written research question identifies the issue being investigated, the population or case, the key variables or concepts, and the type of answer the study seeks. It gives the project boundaries.

🎯 Why It Matters

Your research question affects nearly every later decision: methodology, sample size, instruments, analysis plan, and final discussion. A weak question creates problems throughout the entire project.

📝 It's Provisional

Research questions are provisional in early stages. As you read more sources, compare theories, and test feasibility, you may need to refine the wording. This is completely normal.

Types

Types of Research Questions

Choose your type before planning — it determines wording, evidence, and logic.

📊 Quantitative

Measuring Variables

Focus on measurable variables, numerical data, and patterns that can be tested or described statistically.

  • Descriptive — What exists, how often it occurs
  • Comparative — Differences between groups or conditions
  • Relationship-based — Associations or trends between variables
🗣️ Qualitative

Understanding Meaning

Explore meanings, experiences, processes, beliefs, contexts, or social dynamics. Usually flexible and open-ended.

  • Exploratory — Limited prior research areas
  • Explanatory — Why a process occurs
  • Evaluative — How a program is experienced
  • Emancipatory — Knowledge supporting action
🔀 Mixed-Methods

Integrating Both

Combine quantitative and qualitative approaches. Numbers show the pattern; qualitative evidence explains why it exists.

  • Separate quantitative + qualitative questions, OR
  • Integrated question connecting both strands
Research ApproachBest Used When You Need ToTypical Wording
Quantitative Measure variables, compare groups, test relationships, or analyze numerical trends What is the relationship between X and Y among Z?
Qualitative Understand experiences, meanings, perceptions, practices, or processes in context How do participants describe or experience X?
Mixed-Methods Combine numerical patterns with deeper explanation from interviews or observations How do quantitative outcomes and participant experiences together explain X?
Process

6 Steps to Developing a Good Research Question

From broad interest to focused, researchable inquiry.

1

Start with a Broad Topic

Choose a subject area large enough to explore but not so unfamiliar that you can't understand the literature. Brainstorm subtopics, create concept maps, or discuss with colleagues. Check that the topic is active in current scholarship — active conversations reveal gaps, debates, and unresolved questions.

2

Conduct Preliminary Research

This first review doesn't need to be a full systematic review, but should be organized enough to show patterns. Look for recent studies, review articles, limitations sections, and calls for future research. These reveal underexplored groups, outdated assumptions, inconsistent findings, and contexts needing investigation.

3

Narrow Into Possible Questions

Turn your broad topic into several candidate questions. Use "gap-spotting" — building questions from limitations in prior research — or problematization, which challenges assumptions behind existing theories. Professional experience can also reveal patterns worth studying.

4

Test with the FINER Criteria

Evaluate each candidate question against Feasibility, Interest, Novelty, Ethics, and Relevance before choosing one. A question that fails on feasibility or ethics may prevent the study from being conducted at all.

5

Use a Framework to Structure the Final Question

Frameworks like PICOT, PEO, SPIDER, and CLIP help you avoid vague wording by forcing you to identify essential parts: population, intervention, comparison, outcome, and timeframe.

6

Ensure It Fits the Paper Structure

The research question should guide every major section of the paper — title, abstract, introduction, method, results, and discussion. If a section doesn't help answer the question, it may be unnecessary or misdirected.

Criteria

The FINER Criteria

A practical checklist for judging whether a research question is suitable — introduced by Hulley et al.

Feasible
Can this study be completed with your time, skills, data access, funding, and ethical approvals?
I
Interesting
Will you and your intended audience care about the answer? Sustained interest helps you complete the work.
N
Novel
Does the question add, extend, test, or clarify knowledge — rather than merely repeat existing work?

Ethical
Can the study be conducted without harming participants or violating ethical review standards?
R
Relevant

Frameworks

Question-Building Frameworks

Structured approaches that force clarity by identifying key components of your study.

PICOT

Best for: Clinical & Intervention Research

Introduced by Richardson et al. Used when a study compares an intervention, indicator, or condition against another group or baseline.

P — Population I — Intervention/Indicator C — Comparison O — Outcome T — Timeframe
Among adults with Type 2 diabetes (P), does a low-carb diet (I) compared to a standard diet (C) reduce HbA1c levels (O) within 6 months (T)?
PEO

Best for: Qualitative & Observational Studies

Better when the study is qualitative and focuses on how a population experiences an exposure or condition.

P — Population E — Exposure O — Outcome
How do first-generation college students (P) who experienced food insecurity (E) describe the impact on their academic performance (O)?
SPIDER

Best for: Qualitative Evidence Synthesis

Used for studies where experience or perception is central to the inquiry.

S — Sample PI — Phenomenon of Interest D — Design E — Evaluation R — Research Type
CLIP

Best for: Service Delivery & Applied Research

Useful for practice improvement and applied professional research contexts.

C — Client Group L — Location of Service I — Improvement/Information/Innovation P — Professionals
FrameworkElementsBest Fit
PICOTPopulation, Intervention, Comparison, Outcome, TimeframeClinical research, intervention studies, evidence-based practice
PEOPopulation, Exposure, OutcomeQualitative & observational studies on experiences or exposure
SPIDERSample, Phenomenon of Interest, Design, Evaluation, Research TypeQualitative evidence synthesis and perception-centered studies

Examples

Good vs. Bad Research Questions

Specificity, evidence, and scope make the difference.

Example 1: Too Broad vs. Focused

❌ Weak

"How does social media affect people's behavior?"

Too broad — "social media" and "people's behavior" could refer to countless platforms, groups, and outcomes.

✅ Stronger

"What effect does the daily use of YouTube have on the attention span of children aged under 16?"

Names the platform, behavior of interest, and population.

Example 2: Yes/No vs. Analytical

❌ Weak

"Has there been an increase in childhood obesity in the US in the past 10 years?"

Can be answered with a simple yes or no — requires no analysis.

✅ Stronger

"How have school intervention programs and parental education levels affected the rate of childhood obesity among 1st to 6th-grade students?"

Requires analysis of interventions, parental education, and obesity rates. Invites explanation.

Example 3: Method Posing as a Question

❌ Weak

"Can I conduct interviews with teachers about online learning?"

Describes a method, not a research problem.

✅ Stronger

"How do middle school teachers describe the challenges of maintaining student engagement during online learning?"

Identifies the group, setting, phenomenon, and type of evidence needed.

Pitfalls

Common Pitfalls to Avoid

Many research problems begin with a question that seems reasonable but fails under closer review.

Common MistakeWhy It Weakens the StudyBetter Approach
Question is too broad The project becomes unfocused and collects unrelated evidence Define the population, context, variable, concept, or time frame
Not feasible Researcher may lack access to participants, data, funding, or time Confirm access and resources before finalizing the question
Vague wording Readers don't understand what is being measured or compared Replace abstract terms with precise concepts that can be studied
Skipping the literature review The study may repeat what has already been answered Review current and foundational scholarship before deciding on final wording
Ignoring ethical concerns The study may risk harm, violate privacy, or fail institutional review Consider participant risk, consent, confidentiality, and review board expectations early
Confusing contribution with question "This study will improve policy" doesn't identify what will be investigated Ask a question evidence can answer, then explain the contribution later
Framing a method as the question "Can I survey students?" identifies a tool, not a research problem Ask what the survey will help you discover, measure, compare, or explain

✅ Final Checklist

  • Can this be answered with evidence rather than opinion alone?
  • Have I reviewed enough current and foundational literature?
  • Does the wording clearly identify the population, setting, or phenomenon?
  • Is the scope realistic for my assignment or timeline?
  • Do I have access to the data, participants, or documents needed?
  • Does the question require ethical review or consent protections?
  • Does the question align with my objective, method, and expected outcome?

💡 Key Reminders

  • Expect revision — a strong question often emerges after several rounds of reading and narrowing
  • Stay current — track recent studies and methodological changes
  • Use concise wording — remove terms that don't affect meaning
  • Seek expert input — mentors and librarians can spot problems you miss
  • Don't mistake method for question
  • Don't write anticipated contribution as the question
Alignment

Aligning Your Question with Study Objectives

Your research question should match the objective, expected outcomes, and methods — misalignment creates confusing papers.

1

State the Main Objective

Write one sentence explaining what the study aims to accomplish.

2

Identify the Expected Outcome

Decide what type of result would answer the question — a measured difference, described experience, observed pattern, or tested relationship.

3

Match Wording to Method

Use quantitative wording for measurement, qualitative wording for meaning and experience, and mixed-methods wording when both are integrated.

4

Check Every Major Section

Literature review, sample, data collection, analysis, and discussion should all help answer the same question.

❌ Misaligned Example

Objective: To assess the impact of diet on athletic performance.

Question: How do athletes perceive dietary guidelines?

This explores perceptions, not the direct effect of diet. It may be valid qualitatively, but it doesn't match the stated objective.

✅ Aligned Example

Objective: To assess the impact of diet on athletic performance.

Question: How does adherence to a high-protein diet affect endurance levels in marathon runners?

Names the dietary factor, outcome, and population — gives the study a clear path from question to method to findings.

Why Alignment Matters

🎯
Clarity

Readers quickly understand what the study is trying to answer

🔍
Focus

Avoid collecting evidence that doesn't serve the project

💡
Usability

Findings are easier to interpret because they respond directly to the stated objective

✅
Credibility

Reviewers see a logical connection between question, design, and conclusions
